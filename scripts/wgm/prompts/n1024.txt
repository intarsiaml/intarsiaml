Backpressure in streaming pipelines

Every streaming pipeline is a chain of stages with different speeds. A parser can read two hundred thousand lines a second, an enrichment step that calls a remote service may manage four thousand, and a database writer may only commit in batches of five hundred every few hundred milliseconds. If nothing limits how fast the first stage may push, the slowest stage decides what happens next, and it usually decides badly: queues between stages grow without bound, memory climbs, latency for newly arrived records stretches from milliseconds to minutes, and eventually the process is killed by the operating system with a pile of unacknowledged work still in flight.

Backpressure is the family of techniques that lets a slow consumer tell a fast producer to slow down. There are three common shapes. The first is a bounded queue with blocking: when the queue between two stages is full, the producer's put call simply waits. This is easy to reason about and works well inside one process, but the wait propagates upstream only as far as the threads share the queue, and a blocked thread cannot do useful work such as sending heartbeats. The second is credit-based flow control, used by many network protocols: the consumer grants the producer a number of credits, each credit permits one message, and the producer stops when it has none. Credits can be returned in batches, which keeps the control traffic small, and the consumer can shrink the window when it is struggling. The third is load shedding: when the pipeline cannot keep up, drop the least valuable records on purpose, such as stale telemetry samples, rather than delay everything.

Choosing among them depends on what a dropped or delayed record costs. A payment event must never be dropped, so the pipeline should block and let the pressure travel all the way back to the source, which in turn might be a message broker that is happy to hold a backlog on disk. A metrics sample that is already ten seconds old has little value, and shedding it is the right call. Many systems combine the two: a small bounded queue for smoothing bursts, credits for normal flow, and a shedding policy that only engages above a high-water mark and disengages below a low-water mark, so that it does not flap.

A short example makes the difference concrete. Suppose a pipeline has a reader, a transformer and a writer, and the writer can sustain 500 records a second. With an unbounded queue the reader keeps pushing at 2,000 records a second, so the backlog grows by 1,500 records every second. After ten minutes there are 900,000 records waiting, each of which has been sitting in memory for as long as nine minutes before it is written. With a bounded queue of 1,000 records the reader is held to the writer's pace after the first half second, memory stays flat, and the oldest record is never more than two seconds old. The cost is that the source must be able to wait, which is not true of every source: a UDP listener or a sensor gateway cannot be paused, and for those the only honest choices are a large buffer on disk or shedding.

Credit windows deserve a little more care than they usually get. A window that is too small starves the consumer, because every message pays a full round trip waiting for credit; a window that is too large recreates the unbounded queue one level down. A reasonable starting point is the consumer's throughput multiplied by the round trip time, plus some headroom for jitter, and it should be adjusted from measurements rather than guessed. Returning credits one at a time is wasteful, so most implementations return them when the consumer has processed a quarter or a half of the window.

Backpressure is also hard to test, because it only shows up when something is slow. A useful habit is to put a deliberately slow stage into every integration test and assert that memory and queue ages stay bounded. The same test should also kill the slow stage halfway and check that the upstream stages stop cleanly instead of hanging.

Teams often forget two practical details. First, timeouts interact badly with backpressure: if a producer gives up after thirty seconds of waiting and retries, the retries add load to a system that is already saturated. Second, measuring the right thing matters. Average queue depth hides the problem; the useful signals are the age of the oldest item in each queue, the time producers spend blocked, and the rate at which credits are granted.
